How Many People Speak Chaldean? Where They Live and Why to Learn It

Chaldean Neo-Aramaic, which most speakers simply call Soorath (also written Sureth or Soureth), is a living form of Aramaic, the language family of the ancient Near East. Today it survives mostly in families, in churches and in the diaspora, and in many homes it is slipping away within one generation. Here is what we know about how many people speak it, where they live, and why it is worth learning now.

How many people speak Chaldean?

The honest answer: nobody knows exactly. No census counts Chaldean speakers, and being Chaldean is not the same as speaking Chaldean. Many Chaldeans speak Arabic at home, and many diaspora children understand some Soorath but cannot hold a conversation. The best available numbers:

EstimateFigureSource
Speakers of Chaldean Neo-Aramaicabout 216,000Ethnologue (1994 figure, now very dated) [1]
Members of the Chaldean Catholic Church worldwideabout 419,000CNEWA [2]
Chaldeans in the United Statesmore than 500,000Chaldean News, community estimate (2020) [3]

These figures measure different things (speakers, church members, ethnic community) and come from different years, which is why they don't add up. Treat them as a range, not a precise count. Fluent speakers are certainly a smaller group than the total Chaldean population.

Where do Chaldeans live today?

Iraq, the homeland. Soorath is traditionally spoken in the Christian towns of the Nineveh Plains in northern Iraq, such as Telkeppe (Tel Keppe), Alqosh, Batnaya and Tesqopa, as well as in Baghdad, Mosul and the Kurdistan Region. But Iraq's Christian community has shrunk dramatically: commonly cited estimates say it fell from about 1.5 million in 2003 to roughly 150,000 today [4]. Most Chaldeans now live outside Iraq.

Metro Detroit, Michigan, the largest community outside Iraq. A 2023 Walsh College survey with the Chaldean American Chamber of Commerce estimated about 183,500 Chaldeans in Metro Detroit, up from 160,000 in 2018 and 113,000 in 2008 [5]. Common areas include West Bloomfield, Sterling Heights, Farmington Hills and Commerce Township.

There are also communities in Chicago, Canada, Sweden, Germany and other parts of Europe, and in Jordan, Lebanon and Turkey, where many families waited as refugees.

Why learn Chaldean?

1. To talk with your grandparents while you still can. For many families, the grandparents are the last fluent speakers. Every conversation in Soorath is something no recording can replace.

2. Because the language is at risk. With the homeland communities scattered across the world, Soorath is passed on mainly at home. If one generation stops speaking it, the chain breaks.

3. To understand your faith and culture from the inside. Prayers, hymns, proverbs, wedding songs and family sayings carry meaning that gets lost in translation.

4. To give it to your children. Kids pick up languages fastest when they are young. Even a few hundred words and everyday phrases give them a real connection to who they are.

5. Because it is Aramaic. Soorath belongs to the same language family that was spoken across the ancient Near East for thousands of years. Few people anywhere can say they speak a living Aramaic language.
